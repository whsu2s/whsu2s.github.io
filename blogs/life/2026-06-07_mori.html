十五年前沒教的事 15 Years Ago: What Was Not Taught Then

Posted on 07 June, 2026

剛到德國生活時，每天忙於課業和適應新的環境，晚上則有了探索廚藝和與自己相處的時間。 其中《名偵探柯南》常常是我晚餐的配菜。 雖然是推理動漫，裡面卻不只有殺人案件和解謎過程，也會出現一些科普知識，或是值得反覆思考的人生課題。

毛利小五郎在劇中大多時候是個迷糊又愛喝酒的大叔，但偶爾也會展現出成熟而睿智的一面。 其中讓我印象深刻的是第150–151集〈汽車爆炸事件的真相〉。 案件中的嫌犯崎原惠曾是毛利大學時期擔任家教時教過的學生，是個法律高材生。 因為多年來無法放下心中的仇恨與執念，她讓妹妹坐上被人安裝炸彈的車替她倒車，利用他人之手害死了自己的妹妹。 當真相被毛利揭露後，崎原惠承認犯案，而毛利並沒有急著責備她，反而說了一句耐人尋味的話：

「要怪只怪，十五年前我沒有教妳一件比考大學更重要的事，這件事比起讀書要重要多了。」

毛利指的到底是什麼事呢？ 多年以來我才漸漸體會，那是決定一個人在面對高山低谷各種環境都能好好生活的能力。

相較於我過去熟悉的教育環境，德國的大學課程更重視理解、思辨與表達。 除了期末專案外，有些課程甚至以口試為主，教授要求學生用專業術語回答整個學期所學的的相關問題。 課堂中也常常出現老師與學生之間的討論，而不只是單方面的授課。 學生不僅要知道答案，更要能清楚表達自己的想法，甚至去發現問題。 這讓我逐漸意識到，真正影響人生的能力有很多層面，知識是表層的，表達溝通力是更深層面的，像是面對人與人之間的誤會與衝突。

從小到大，師長都會教我們要懂得說「請」、「謝謝」、「對不起」。 然而，當問題不是表示禮貌或是一句道歉就能解決呢？ 當誤會已經累積許久，當委屈、憤怒或不滿藏在心裡多年，當彼此都覺得自己有道理時，我們該如何表達自己的感受？ 又該如何理解對方的立場？ 這些問題往往沒有標準答案，但卻真實存在於每個人的生活裡。

許多人從小到大接受了十幾年的教育，卻未必學過如何進行困難的對話。 當關係出現裂痕時，有人選擇壓抑，有人選擇逃避，也有人把情緒累積成怨恨。 久而久之，問題沒有消失，只是被埋藏起來，等待某一天以更激烈的方式爆發。

崎原惠的悲劇，其實正是如此。 她不是因為不夠聰明而犯案，而是因為被仇恨困住太久，始終無法與自己的情緒和解。 而這樣的能力，在異鄉生活時顯得更加重要。 當一個人住在陌生的國家，遇到的問題不再只是考試或作業。 還要學習如何與不同文化背景的人相處，在語言不熟悉的情況下表達需求，面對孤獨、挫折與不確定感。 有時候也要懂得相信，去接受別人伸出的援手。 真正支撐一個人的不是過去的成績，而是面對生活的能力和韌性。

回頭再看毛利小五郎那句話，不僅提醒了他的學生和他自己，也提醒了我。 知識和專業能讓我們在社會上立足，但表達自己、處理情緒、化解衝突、面對挫折，以及與他人建立健康的關係，這些能力才是陪伴我們一輩子的功課。

When I first moved to Germany, my days were filled with coursework and adjusting to a completely new environment. In the evenings, I found time to explore cooking and learn how to enjoy my own company. During many of those dinners, Detective Conan was my constant companion. Although it is known as a detective anime, the series offers much more than murder cases and clever deductions. Alongside the mysteries, it often weaves in bits of scientific knowledge and life lessons that are worth revisiting and reflecting on time and time again.

Most of the time in the series, Mori Kogoro comes across as a scatterbrained, hard-drinking middle-aged man. Yet every now and then, he reveals a side of himself that is remarkably mature and wise. One moment that has stayed with me comes from Episodes 150–151, "The Truth Behind the Car Explosion Case." In this case, the culprit, Megumi Sakihara, had once been Mori’s student when he worked as a private tutor during his university years. She was exceptionally talented and went on to study law. Unable to let go of the hatred and obsession she had carried for years, she manipulated events so that her younger sister would get into a car that had been rigged with a bomb. By using someone else’s hand to carry out the act, she ultimately caused her own sister’s death. When Mouri uncovered the truth, Megumi confessed to the crime. Instead of immediately condemning her, he said something deeply thought-provoking:

“If anyone is to blame, then blame me for failing to teach you something fifteen years ago. Something far more important than getting into university. It is far more important than academic success.”

What was this lesson Mori was referring to? It took me many years to gradually understand what he meant. He was talking about a kind of ability that allows a person to live well through ups and downs, the ability to navigate life through both its triumphs and its hardships.

Compared to the educational environment I was familiar with, university courses in Germany placed much greater emphasis on understanding, critical thinking, and communication. In addition to final projects, some courses were even assessed primarily through oral examinations, where professors expected students to answer questions covering an entire semester’s material using appropriate professional terminology. Classrooms also frequently involved discussions between professors and students, rather than one-way lectures. Students were expected not only to know the answers, but also to articulate their ideas clearly and, at times, to identify the questions that had yet to be asked. These experiences gradually made me realize that the abilities that truly shape a person's life exist on many different levels. Knowledge is only the surface layer. Communication and self-expression lie deeper beneath it, influencing how we handle misunderstandings, navigate conflicts, and connect with other people.

From childhood onward, our parents and teachers taught us the importance of saying “please,” “thank you,” and “I’m sorry.” But what happens when a problem cannot be resolved by simple politeness or a single apology? When misunderstandings have been building up for years, when hurt, anger, or resentment have been quietly buried deep inside, and when both sides are convinced that they are right, how do we express what we truly feel? And how do we begin to understand the perspective of the other person? These questions rarely come with clear-cut answers. Yet they are real challenges that exist in every person's life.

Many people spend more than a decade in formal education, yet never truly learn how to engage in difficult conversations. When relationships begin to fracture, some choose to suppress their feelings. Others avoid the issue altogether. Still others allow their emotions to accumulate until disappointment and hurt harden into resentment. Over time, the problem does not disappear. It is merely buried beneath the surface, waiting for the day it erupts in a far more destructive way.

Megumi Sakihara’s tragedy is, in many ways, exactly of this nature. She did not commit the crime because she lacked intelligence, but because she had been trapped in hatred for far too long, never finding a way to reconcile with her own emotions. And this kind of ability becomes even more important when living abroad. When a person resides in a foreign country, the challenges they face are no longer limited to exams or assignments. They must learn how to interact with people from different cultural backgrounds, how to express their needs in an unfamiliar language, and how to face loneliness, frustration, and uncertainty. At times, they must also learn to trust and to accept the helping hand extended by others. In the end, what truly sustains a person is not past academic achievement, but the capacity and resilience to face life itself.

Looking back at Mori’s words, I realize they were not only a reminder to his student and to himself, but also a reminder to me. Knowledge and professional skills may help us find our place in society, but the ability to express ourselves, manage our emotions, resolve conflicts, cope with setbacks, and build healthy relationships with others—these are the lessons that stay with us for a lifetime. It is the case that will be explored throughout our lifetime until it is closed.
